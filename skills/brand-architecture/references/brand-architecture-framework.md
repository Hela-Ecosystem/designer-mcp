# Brand Architecture Framework

## Research basis

The framework treats brand architecture as a portfolio strategy rather than a
logo arrangement. The California Management Review article by David Aaker and
Erich Joachimsthaler describes it as an organizing structure that specifies
brand roles and relationships, and introduces a spectrum for handling sub-brands,
endorsed brands, and alternatives:

- [The Brand Relationship Spectrum, California Management Review / UC Berkeley](https://cmr.berkeley.edu/2000/08/42-4-the-brand-relationship-spectrum-the-key-to-the-brand-architecture-challenge/)

Design Bridge and Partners frames the work as structuring and communicating the
relationship between brands, products, and services, and distinguishes branded
house, house of brands, and hybrid models:

- [What is Brand Architecture? A 2025 Comprehensive Guide](https://www.designbridge.com/insights/brand-architecture-guide)

Vivaldi Group emphasizes that the architecture also governs naming conventions,
visual identity hierarchy, and how equity flows between portfolio entities:

- [Brand Architecture: Types, Models & Frameworks](https://vivaldigroup.com/brand-architecture-types-models-strategic-frameworks/)
- [Brand Portfolio Strategy Consulting](https://vivaldigroup.com/service/brand-product-portfolio-strategy/)

These sources support the decision structure below; they do not replace
customer research, legal clearance, or category-specific analysis.

## Relationship models

| Model | Parent visibility | Use when | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Branded house / monolithic | High; the master brand leads | Shared promise, audience, trust, and experience should compound | One failure or mismatch can travel across the portfolio |
| Sub-brand | High, with a named offer beneath it | A new offer needs distinction but should borrow substantial parent equity | The hierarchy becomes confusing or every feature becomes a brand |
| Endorsed brand | Visible but secondary endorsement | The offer needs its own identity while parent credibility reduces perceived risk | Endorsement becomes decorative or inconsistent |
| House of brands | Low or invisible parent connection | Audiences, promises, channels, or risk profiles need real independence | Duplicated investment and weak portfolio-level leverage |
| Hybrid | Deliberately mixed relationships | A portfolio contains materially different businesses or acquired equity | Exceptions accumulate without governance and become accidental architecture |

Do not pick a model from a slogan. Place each entity on the relationship
spectrum, then explain why the selected visibility and autonomy are appropriate.

## Decision matrix

Score each candidate model from 1–5 for each dimension, then write the reason;
the numbers are a forcing device, not customer research.

| Dimension | Higher score favors more parent connection | Evidence to collect |
|---|---|---|
| Shared audience and job | Same people and decision context | Segments, journeys, sales evidence |
| Shared promise | Same credibility and value proposition | Positioning, proof, service model |
| Parent permission | Parent is trusted in the new category | Awareness, associations, partner feedback |
| Equity leverage | Parent investment should accelerate the offer | Acquisition cost, cross-sell, distribution |
| Reputational coupling | Failure should or should not affect siblings | Safety, regulation, service dependency |
| Differentiation need | Offer requires separation to be understood | Competitive set, price, experience, use case |
| Acquisition autonomy | Existing equity must survive independently | Awareness, contracts, channel strength, culture |
| Governance capacity | Teams can enforce a shared system | Owners, budget, review cadence, tooling |

The output must include the evidence gaps and the rejected model with the most
credible alternative, not only the winning score.

## Naming hierarchy template

```text
Master / corporate brand
└── Offer family or range
    └── Product or service name
        └── Variant / edition / descriptor
```

Define:

- which levels are proper names versus descriptors;
- word order, capitalization, separators, and abbreviation grammar;
- whether names travel across markets or are localized;
- collision rules for sibling names and feature names;
- trademark, domain, app-store, search, accessibility, and pronunciation checks;
- how acquired names are retained, endorsed, renamed, or retired; and
- the transition period and redirect/alias behavior for changed names.

## Governance matrix

| Decision | Accountable owner | Required evidence | Approval |
|---|---|---|---|
| New offer name | Portfolio/brand owner | Audience, positioning, collision and legal checks | Architecture council or delegate |
| New sub-brand | Business owner + brand owner | Model score, role, investment, risk | Executive sponsor |
| Acquisition treatment | Portfolio owner | Equity, audience, contracts, migration cost | Integration steering group |
| Local exception | Regional owner | Market, language, regulatory reason | Brand governance owner |
| Rename or retirement | Product + brand owner | Customer impact, migration, support plan | Change authority |

Document the exception expiry date. An exception without an owner or expiry is
an ungoverned second architecture.

## Measurement plan

Choose measures that test the intended relationship, with a baseline and owner:

- findability and correct portfolio navigation;
- recognition of parent endorsement where it is intended;
- distinctiveness where independence is intended;
- cross-offer comprehension and qualified progression;
- duplicate naming, design, and campaign effort;
- migration adoption, support contacts, redirect success, and retention; and
- compliance with naming and expression rules over time.

Do not claim increased equity or revenue from architecture alone. Record the
mechanism and competing factors that could explain any change.
